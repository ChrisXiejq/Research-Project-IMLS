\begin{abstract}
Motion prediction and risk allocation are commonly evaluated in isolation,
although their benefit in autonomous driving is realised through a coupled
prediction--planning--supervision stack. This dissertation studies a
controlled give-way intersection in CARLA and asks whether task adaptation,
explicit interaction-sequence modelling and adaptive risk allocation improve
offline prediction and closed-loop safety--efficiency outcomes. A
rollout-disjoint dataset of 200 factorial rollouts is used to compare a
pretrained MultiPath control, a simple task-adapted prediction head,
capacity-matched multilayer perceptron controls and two Transformer adapters
under a validation-frozen, one-shot test protocol. Simple task adaptation

\subsubsection*{Broader impact statement}
This work evaluates autonomous-driving components only in a controlled CARLA
scenario and must not be interpreted as evidence of real-road safety. A
failure mode identified here is particularly relevant to deployment: global
calibration may improve aggregate likelihood while worsening rare
response-active cases. Reporting coupled solver and supervisor behaviour,
negative findings and population limits reduces the risk that an offline
metric improvement is presented as a safety guarantee. The released evidence
contains simulator trajectories and metrics rather than personal data.
